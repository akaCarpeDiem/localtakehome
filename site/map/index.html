Why take-home pay varies by state

At $80,000, a single filer pays the same federal income tax ($8,770) and the same Social Security and Medicare ($6,120) in every state. Every difference on this list comes from state income tax and state payroll programs. In states with no wage tax, such as Alaska, Florida, Nevada, and Texas, the estimate is about $65,110 a year. At the bottom of the list, Oregon, Hawaii, and California keep roughly $58,860 to $60,860. In those states, relatively high rates start at modest incomes, and California adds its 1.3% SDI payroll premium.

What this map does not show

  • Local wage taxes. City and county taxes are left out so every state is compared the same way. In places like New York City, Philadelphia, or Detroit, a local tax can lower net pay by another 2% to 4% of wages. The local taxes guide lists them.
  • Other filing statuses and incomes. The order can change at other incomes. Flat-tax states compare differently with graduated states as income rises.
  • Cost of living. A state with no income tax may have higher property or sales taxes, housing, or insurance costs. None of those are on a paycheck.

Questions

Which state has the highest take-home pay?

At the same salary, eight states without a wage income tax tie at the top: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, and Wyoming. Washington also has no wage income tax, but at $80,000 it keeps about $1,110 less because of its paid leave and WA Cares premiums.

Why does a no-tax state still take 18% of my pay?

Federal income tax and FICA apply everywhere. At $80,000, they come to about $14,890 for a single filer before any state tax.
