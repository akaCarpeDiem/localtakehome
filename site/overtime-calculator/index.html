How overtime is paid and taxed

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), non-exempt employees must be paid at least one and a half times their regular rate for every hour over 40 in a workweek (U.S. Department of Labor, overtime pay). Some states require more. California, for example, also requires overtime after eight hours in a single workday. Salaried employees who meet the FLSA tests for exempt status are generally not owed overtime.

There is no special tax rate for overtime on a paycheck. Overtime is regular wages. It is added to the rest of your pay and withheld at your normal federal and state rates, plus 6.2% Social Security and 1.45% Medicare. A big overtime week can look heavily taxed because payroll systems treat that one check as if every check that year were the same size. The extra withholding usually evens out when you file your return.

Example: $28 an hour with 5 overtime hours a week

Single filer in Texas, paid weekly, 40 regular hours plus 5 overtime hours at 1.5× every week. Overtime pays $42 an hour, so the 5 hours add $210 a week, or $10,920 over 52 weeks. Gross pay rises from $58,240 to $69,160. Federal income tax rises by about $1,576 and Social Security and Medicare by about $835. That leaves about $8,508 more take-home pay for the year, or $163.62 more per weekly check. That is roughly 78 cents of each overtime dollar.

The 2025–2028 federal overtime deduction

The 2025 federal tax law created a deduction for qualified overtime compensation for tax years 2025 through 2028. It covers only the “half” in time-and-a-half, meaning the premium above your regular rate that the FLSA requires, not your whole overtime pay. The deduction is up to $12,500 per return ($25,000 for married couples filing jointly). It shrinks once modified adjusted gross income passes $150,000 ($300,000 joint). Starting in 2026, employers report qualified overtime separately on Form W-2 in box 12 with code TT. In the example above, the premium is $14 × 5 hours × 52 weeks, or $3,640 for the year.

The deduction lowers federal income tax on your return. It does not lower Social Security or Medicare. Employers keep withholding as usual unless you update your Form W-4 (Step 4(b), deductions). This calculator shows the paycheck as it is normally withheld, so it does not subtract the deduction. Your tax bill at filing time may be lower than your withholding suggests. Details are in the IRS questions and answers on the overtime deduction.

Questions

Is overtime taxed more than regular pay?

No. It is taxed at the same rates as the rest of your wages. Extra withholding on a large overtime check is a payroll timing effect, and it is settled when you file your return.

Does “no tax on overtime” mean nothing is withheld from my overtime?

No. For 2025 through 2028, eligible workers can deduct the overtime premium (up to $12,500, or $25,000 joint) on their federal return. Overtime is still subject to withholding, Social Security, and Medicare, and usually to state income tax.

Who is entitled to overtime pay?

Under federal law, non-exempt employees covered by the FLSA must get at least 1.5 times their regular rate for hours over 40 in a workweek. Many salaried professional, executive, and administrative employees are exempt. State law can add daily overtime or other rules.

Why does the calculator spread my overtime over the whole year?

Tax brackets apply to a full year. Treating your typical weekly overtime as part of annual pay gives a steadier estimate of what each check keeps. A single heavy week will have more withheld than this average.
